A web server retries a database request until it succeeds.
Each request takes one second and succeeds independently with probability $1/2$.
Adding a cache takes half a second initially and raises the success probability to $2/3$ <Citation {...references.incremental} locator={undefined} />.
Both designs take two seconds on average, but does the cache make the response time more predictable?

## Expected Runtime

The [`reward`](../../heyvl/statements.md#reward-and-weigh) statement collects the modeled runtime: one second for every request, including the successful one.
Other program statements add no time in this model.
With postexpectation `0`, the [`@wp`](../../heyvl/procs.md) specification bounds the expected accumulated reward.

The invariant `2 * [!done]` bounds the expected remaining runtime by two seconds while requests are still needed.
One iteration incurs one second and leaves another two seconds to be paid with probability $1/2$, giving $1+\tfrac12\cdot2=2$.

In the cached variant, the expected number of requests is $3/2$.
Its half-second initialization cost brings the same upper bound of two seconds.

## Second Moments

For runtime $T$, its second moment $\mathbb{E}[T^2]$ measures the expected squared runtime.
Together with the mean, it determines the variance:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(T)=\mathbb{E}[T^2]-\mathbb{E}[T]^2.
$$

To collect the square incrementally, introduce a fresh ghost variable `tau` that records elapsed time.
The original guards, probabilities, and assignments do not read `tau`.
An additional second increases the square by $(\mathtt{tau}+1)^2-\mathtt{tau}^2=2\cdot\mathtt{tau}+1$.
Replacing `reward 1` by this increment makes the accumulated reward equal $T^2$ on every terminating execution <Citation {...references.incremental} />.

Apply the transformation to every original reward, including initialization costs.

For an unfinished request sequence, the invariant `6 + 4 * tau` bounds the squared-time increments still to come.
One iteration collects `2 * tau + 1` and continues with probability $1/2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(2\cdot\mathtt{tau}+1)\\
&\quad+\tfrac12\cdot\bigl(6+4\cdot(\mathtt{tau}+1)\bigr)\\
&=6+4\cdot\mathtt{tau}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Starting at `tau = 0` gives the verified upper bound of six squared seconds.
On a nonterminating execution, these incremental rewards also grow without bound.
A single `reward tau * tau` after the loop would never be reached on that execution and would collect no reward.

For the cached design, `tau` starts at `0.5` and `reward 0.25` accounts for the square of the initialization time.
The loop uses the same increments, with an invariant adapted to the larger success probability.

## Comparing the Designs

The procedures above prove upper bounds on the first two moments.
To determine the variance, we also need matching lower bounds.
We omit those proofs here and use the exact moment values calculated below <Citation {...references.incremental} />.

If each request succeeds independently with probability $0<p\le1$, the number $K$ of requests up to and including the first success satisfies

$$
\mathbb{E}[K]=\frac1p,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[K^2]=\frac{2-p}{p^2}.
$$

<RetryLatencyPlot>

Without caching, $T=K$ and $p=1/2$.
With caching, $T=1/2+K$ and $p=2/3$, so $\mathbb{E}[T^2]=1/4+\mathbb{E}[K]+\mathbb{E}[K^2]$.

At 1 second, half the uncached requests have finished, while cached requests take at least 1.5 seconds.
At 2.5 seconds, $1/4$ of uncached requests are still waiting, compared with $1/9$ of cached requests.

</RetryLatencyPlot>

| Design | Mean runtime | Second moment | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Without a cache | $2$ s | $6$ s$^2$ | $2$ s$^2$ |
| With a cache | $2$ s | $4.75$ s$^2$ | $0.75$ s$^2$ |

The cache reduces the variance while preserving the mean.
